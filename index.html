How the registry works

Method

One claim, one schema, one standard of proof — applied the same way to every entry, including the ones we'd like to be true. These are the rules that keep the registry honest as it grows.

Peer-reviewedexperts read the paper before it was published — not that it's correct

What this is, and isn't

This is a record of scientific claims, the evidence under each, and the specific test that would settle it. It is not a list of things that are false, and not a forum. A claim appears here because its test hasn't been run or its evidence has been misread downstream — not because someone dislikes it. The one rule that makes it different from an opinion site: every claim, including every competing hypothesis, goes through the same six fields and the same test standard. A challenger idea gets no free pass for being the challenger.

The six-field entry

  1. Claim as stated

    Quoted from the paper, with a citation. A separate line records how it's been reported publicly when that differs from the paper.

  2. Evidence base

    What the study actually did: design, sample, and what was measured.

  3. Untested assumption or unaddressed alternative

    The specific gap, stated neutrally — what wasn't measured or ruled out. No guesses about motive.

  4. What would settle it

    The concrete test, with the predicted result if the claim holds and if it doesn't. When the move is re-running an existing study, the entry labels it a required replication.

  5. Current status

    Replications, published critiques, author responses, and whether the test has been run.

  6. Entry author and conflicts

    Who wrote the entry and any stake they have in the outcome.

Evidence tiers

Claims sit at different strengths of evidence, and most drift happens when one strength is quietly swapped for another. This ladder is universal — it works for biology, AI, physics, economics, anything. Each entry then defines what the tiers mean concretely for its own field, and collecting that domain-specific version is part of writing the entry. Entry 001 spells out the prion version: present in a sample → acts in cultured cells → seeds an effect in an animal → causes clinical disease.

  • T1Observed or associated. The pattern is seen, or two things correlate. No controlled test yet.
  • T2Shown under controlled or model conditions. Demonstrated in a lab, a simulation, or a simplified stand-in for the real thing.
  • T3Shown in the real system, partially. Demonstrated in the actual target, but limited — small scale, one setting, or preliminary.
  • T4Established in the real system, measured. The full claim demonstrated in the real-world target, with a quantified result others can reproduce.

How an entry is scored

Three separate questions, never collapsed into one. Each has its own vocabulary so a reader can't mistake "we checked the quote" for "an expert signed off" for "the science is settled."

Per-source tags

Peer-reviewedExperts read the manuscript before publication. Not a claim that anyone repeated the study.

Entry flags

ActiveThe claim is cited in current work.
LegacyAn older open question, often one that current work quietly rests on.
ContestedTwo credible groups disagree on the right test; the entry shows both and picks neither.

What makes a good test

  1. Concrete and pre-specified.

    It names the measurement, the design, and the threshold — before anyone runs it.

  2. Symmetric.

    It states what result would support the claim and what result would sink it. One-sided isn't a test.

  3. Runnable in principle.

    A lab could actually do it, and the entry names roughly what that would take.

  4. It has a checkable outcome.

    A test names a result to look for ahead of time, then checks whether it appears. "This deserves more study" is a fine starting point — the entry's job is to turn it into a test by naming what result would answer it.

Writing neutrally

Bias usually enters through word choice, not claims. The tables below are the house style: the left column is what to avoid, the right is what to write instead. This guide is public on purpose — readers can hold every entry to it.

Words that assume motive

AvoidUse instead
willfully ignored, masked, hid, suppressednot addressed, not measured, not reported
wants the public to believeconcludes, states, reports
overlooked, missedoutside the study's scope, not tested in this design

Words that close an open question

AvoidUse instead
proven, verified, confirmedsupported by, consistent with, replicated in
debunked, disproven, mythnot supported by, contradicted by [study]
fact, settled, establishedwidely accepted, current consensus

Words that put people on teams

AvoidUse instead
mainstream, establishment, institutionalthe study, the authors, the field's current consensus
Big Pharma, industry-funded (as a dismissal)funded by [X], per the conflicts section
skeptics, believers, so-called, allegedresearchers who propose [alternative], or drop it

Words that add heat

AvoidUse instead
shocking, alarming, troublingnotable, unresolved
crisis, scandalopen question, replication gap
always, never, completelyin [n] of [n] studies, in this sample
The test for any entry: could the study's authors read it and agree it describes their work accurately, even if they dislike the conclusion? If yes, it's neutral enough.

Conflicts, provenance, and honesty

  • 01Every entry names its author and their stake. Any paid relationship touching a claim is disclosed on the entry.
  • 02Quotes are marked located or verified at source. AI-assisted research is disclosed, with reviewer and date.
  • 03Peer-reviewed is not the same as true. Verified is not the same as true. The badges describe process, not correctness — seven peer-reviewed papers can still carry a drifted claim.
  • 04Paid review work is firewalled from public status. Paying never changes a rating, and this page says so plainly.

Every claim is a star. The more it connects — to its domains, and to the claims that rest on it — the brighter it burns. Color says what kind of star it is, so a bright one is never mistaken for a true one.

Labeled stars are real entries — click one to open it · the rest are illustrative

Color — the claim's evidence state:
Yellow — popular hypothesis, never tested. Bright from attention, hollow underneath.

Two ways to read it

The overview sky

The whole registry at a glance. Claims cluster into their domains, so you can see where a field is crowded, where it's quiet, and which corners glow with popular-but-untested ideas. Zoom from a domain down to a sub-science and then a single claim. This is the front door — the sense of the whole.

A claim's constellation

Click any star to center it and see only what it touches: the claims it rests on, the ones that cite it, and the domains it spans. Small enough to read no matter how large the registry grows — the working view, for tracing one claim and finding the open tests around it.

Why it's a real-build feature

A live star map needs a database and a graph engine to compute every claim's connections and redraw as the registry grows — more than a single page can hold. The groundwork is already here: every entry stores its domain memberships and its links to other entries, which are exactly the edges this map will draw. The labeled stars above are already drawn from that data — each real entry, colored by its hypothesis status and joined to the entries it rests on. Why this exists

Most confusion isn't a shortage of information — it's an excess of noise. Data, headlines, and urgent claims pile up faster than anyone can check them, and the original finding, the actual study and the real measurement, ends up buried under layers of citation that drift further from the source with each repetition.

Assumption Check is a flashlight for that dark. It traces a claim back to what was actually measured, marks how far the checking has gone, and names the one test that would settle it. Nothing is called true for being popular, peer-reviewed, or often repeated — only when the evidence holds.

The tools to do this at scale arrived just in time. Recovering a buried source and mapping how a claim drifted across a literature once took a specialist weeks; it can now be done carefully, in the open, and checked by anyone.

Core values

Identity

Every claim, source, and reviewer is named. You always know what you're looking at, and who stands behind it.

Transparency

The method, the wording rules, and each entry's confidence level are public — so the work can be held to its own standard.

Integrity

Findings follow the evidence, not funding, popularity, or pressure. Paid review never changes a public rating.

How we stay honest

  • 01Every entry names its author and any stake they hold. The full conflict and provenance rules are public on the Method page.
  • 02The paid review service (Assumption Check for labs) runs separately from the public registry. Paying for a review never changes a claim's public status.
  • 03Claims are scored the same way regardless of who holds them or how widely they're believed — and the score says how far the checking has gone, never that a claim is simply "true."
