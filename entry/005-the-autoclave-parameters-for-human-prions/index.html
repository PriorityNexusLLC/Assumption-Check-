The autoclave parameters for human prions

The two numbers recited worldwide for prion decontamination — 134°C for 18 minutes, and 1 M sodium hydroxide for an hour — come from a 1999 WHO consultation that lists no inactivation experiment behind them. What the readable bioassays show is that a single steam cycle does not fully inactivate human prions, and that the treatments which do work are chemical or combined.

Claim as stated

The recommended steam-sterilisation parameters (134°C for 18 minutes) inactivate human prions on surgical instruments.

Claim as stated

The claim, as it appears in biosafety manuals worldwide, is that steam sterilisation at 134°C for 18 minutes (often paired with 1 M sodium hydroxide or 20,000 ppm hypochlorite for an hour) inactivates prions on reusable surgical instruments. The parameters trace to one document: the WHO Infection Control Guidelines for Transmissible Spongiform Encephalopathies, from a consultation held in March 1999. That document lists the 134°C / 18-minute cycle as one of its decontamination options and introduces all of them with the sentence: "The following recommendations are based on the best available evidence at this time and are listed in order of more to less severe treatments."

Evidence base

The WHO guidance sets out the options in Annex III. Option 6 is "Autoclave at 134°C for 18 minutes," carried with a footnote conceding that "in worse-case scenarios (brain tissue bake-dried on to surfaces) infectivity will be largely but not completely removed." No primary inactivation experiment is cited for the 18-minute figure, the 1 M NaOH concentration or the 20,000 ppm chlorine figure; the footnotes attached to them are definitional (how to make up the solutions) and the document's only citation note points to other WHO reports. The guidance's own statement of its evidence base, given for the tissue-infectivity table, is that the data are "incomplete" and rest on "primates inoculated with tissues from human cases of CJD, but … supplemented in some categories by data obtained from naturally occurring animal TSEs."

What the readable bioassays since then show is a split result: chemical treatments and combination (chemical-then-autoclave) methods abolish infectivity on steel carriers, while a single steam cycle does not fully inactivate at least one sporadic-CJD strain.

Untested assumption

That a single 134°C / 18-minute steam cycle reduces human-prion infectivity to zero. No published infectivity (bioassay) experiment verified for this entry establishes that for the 18-minute figure specifically; the figure concedes incomplete removal in its own footnote, and the modern bioassay data on single cycles run against complete inactivation for sporadic-CJD material.

Evidence tiers

What T1–T4 mean for a decontamination parameter.

  • T1 The treatment reduces a surrogate marker (residual surface protein, or seeding activity) under laboratory conditions.
  • T2 The treatment inactivates a model prion (hamster or mouse scrapie) on a carrier, read by animal bioassay.
  • T3 The treatment inactivates human prions on a carrier, read by bioassay — shown for some treatments.
  • T4 The treatment reduces human-prion infectivity to zero under realistic reprocessing conditions, measured.

The claim above is a T4 claim. The readable evidence reaches T3 for chemical and combination treatments (Bélondrade 2020; Mohri 2026). A single 134°C steam cycle does not reach T4 for sporadic-CJD material: Mohri 2026 recovered residual infectivity after it.

What would settle it

  • If the claim holds: a bioassay of human-prion-contaminated steel treated at exactly 134°C for 18 minutes, with no transmission to a sensitive host and a titre reduction measured against an untreated dilution series — i.e., a measured floor, not "no detectable signal."
  • If it doesn't: residual transmission, or residual infectivity recoverable by amplification, after the same cycle — which is what the single-cycle data now show for one sporadic strain.

On steel wires carrying sporadic-CJD prions (M1 and V2), single treatments did not eliminate infectivity by bioassay — except autoclaving against the less thermostable V2 strain. What it does not show
It tests sporadic-CJD strains (M1, V2) on wires, not the vCJD agent or the exact 134°C / 18-minute cycle; "practical inactivation" is defined by the assay's detection floor.

Where it stands

  • Shown. The 134°C / 18-minute figure originates in the 1999 WHO guidance (verified at source), which presents it as "best available evidence," cites no inactivation study for it, and concedes incomplete removal in worst cases. Chemical standards (1 N NaOH, 20,000 ppm hypochlorite, one hour) abolish vCJD transmission on steel wires (Bélondrade 2020, verified).
  • Runs against the claim. A single steam cycle does not eliminate sporadic-CJD infectivity on wires for the more resistant strain; a two-step chemical-then-autoclave method does (Mohri 2026, verified).
  • Still open. Whether 134°C / 18 minutes reduces human-prion infectivity to zero.
  • a bioassay dose–response of the exact cycle on human-prion-contaminated steel, reported as a measured floor rather than "no detectable signal"
  • whether the result differs between vCJD and sporadic-CJD strains, and between gravity-displacement and porous-load cycles
  • reading the 1994 Taylor study at source to confirm the incomplete-inactivation figures now carried only from secondary summaries

The 134°C/18-min figure exists in guidance → established (WHO 1999, verified). That a single such cycle fully inactivates human prions → not demonstrated; one sporadic strain survives it (Mohri 2026). That chemical/combination treatments work on steel → supported (Bélondrade 2020; Mohri 2026).
