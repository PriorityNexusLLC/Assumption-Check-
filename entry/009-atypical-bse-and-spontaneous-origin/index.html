Atypical BSE and spontaneous origin

The claim that the two atypical forms of bovine spongiform encephalopathy, H-type and L-type, arise spontaneously in cattle at a low background rate. The international BSE risk framework is built on it. The evidence for it is circumstantial, and its sources describe it as a belief.

Claim as stated

Atypical BSE (H-type and L-type) arises spontaneously in cattle, rather than from an infectious or feed-borne source.

Claim as stated

The World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) Terrestrial Animal Health Code, Chapter 11.4, Article 11.4.1:

"For the purposes of official BSE risk status recognition, BSE excludes 'atypical BSE' as a condition believed to occur spontaneously in all cattle populations at a very low rate."

WOAH's public disease page states the same assumption and, in the same paragraph, a qualification: "whilst to date there is no evidence that atypical BSE is transmissible, recycling of the atypical BSE agent has not been ruled out, and therefore measures to manage exposure risk in the feed chain continue to be recommended as a precautionary measure."

In secondary use the claim is usually shortened to "atypical BSE is spontaneous." The source wording is "believed."

Evidence base

Classical BSE is a feed-borne disease of cattle. Atypical BSE was recognised in the early 2000s, once active testing of slaughtered cattle began, as two molecular signatures distinct from classical BSE: H-type (Biacabe et al. 2004, France) and L-type, also called BASE (Casalone et al. 2004, Italy).

The case for a spontaneous origin is circumstantial. It rests on four observations:

  • Age. Cases have "only been identified in older bovines when conducting intensive surveillance" (WOAH). The two Italian L-type cattle were "older than other affected bovines" in the series (Casalone 2004).
  • Low, steady rate. WOAH describes the yearly incidence as "estimated to be negligible."
  • Independence from the classical epidemic. Cases are reported in countries with and without a history of classical BSE.
  • One genetic case. A 10-year-old H-type cow in Alabama (2006) carried E211K, a PRNP variant at the position corresponding to a pathogenic mutation in human genetic CJD (Richt & Hall 2008). The K211 allele was not detected in any of 6,062 other US cattle screened (Heaton et al. 2008).

Each of these is also consistent with other explanations. Age concentration is what a long incubation from an early-life exposure would produce. A low rate seen only under intensive testing is what any rare condition looks like once testing begins. One heritable case shows a genetic route exists in one animal; it does not show that other cases are spontaneous.

Untested assumption

That cases with no identified exposure arose without one. Spontaneous origin is the residual explanation once a feed or genetic source is not found, and it has not been tested directly. A 2016 review from the German national reference laboratory states that "due to lack of data, the assumptions regarding the spontaneous etiology of both atypical BSE forms (H-BSE and L-BSE) and also the origin of the classical BSE epidemic are still mainly speculative" (Balkema-Buschmann et al. 2016).

A related, separately testable point: in mouse and hamster passage experiments, "both the atypical BSE forms may alter their phenotypes and become indistinguishable from classical BSE" (same review). If that occurs in cattle, atypical BSE is a possible source of classical BSE, which is why WOAH keeps feed controls in place for it.

Evidence tiers

What T1–T4 mean for this claim.

  • T1 Atypical cases are observed with features consistent with a spontaneous disease (old age, low steady rate, no identified exposure).
  • T2 A spontaneous mechanism is shown under model conditions: for example, a PRNP variant or a spontaneous misfolding event producing atypical BSE in an experimental animal.
  • T3 Spontaneous origin is shown for some real cases: a heritable or de novo cause identified in field cases, or exposure excluded case by case.
  • T4 Spontaneous origin is established for atypical BSE generally: a measured, stable incidence in a herd demonstrably unexposed, at the rate the hypothesis predicts.

The claim above is a T4 claim. The evidence reaches T1 for atypical BSE generally, and T3 for one H-type case (E211K). No T4 evidence exists.

What would settle it

  • If the claim holds: a stable incidence of atypical BSE, at about the same age-specific rate, in large herds with no possible feed exposure, across decades and across countries with different feed histories. Brown et al. 2006 set out the scale: "To achieve 95% statistical confidence that the prevalence for sporadic BSE is no greater than 1 per million (i.e., the annual incidence of sporadic Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease [CJD] in humans) would require negative tests in 3 million randomly selected older cattle."
  • If it doesn't: atypical cases cluster by feed source, birth cohort, region or herd; or a transmissible source is identified for cases outside the E211K family.
  • Limits. Brown et al. note that a country guaranteed never to have been exposed "will never be realized," and that "a study of sporadic BSE would only be truly convincing if no cases were identified." The hypothesis is therefore hard to confirm directly; it can more readily be weakened by clustering.

Current status

  • No published study tests the spontaneous hypothesis directly. The international framework treats it as a working assumption.
  • A dedicated published critique of the hypothesis was not found in the research notes (§27 of the Prion Research Notes) and was not found again for this entry.
  • L-type atypical BSE has crossed to human-PrP models: in mice expressing human PrP Met129, "L-type prions appeared to propagate in these mice with no obvious transmission barrier" and "H-type prions failed to infect the mice" (Béringue et al. 2008). A single cynomolgus macaque inoculated intracerebrally with L-type (BASE) had a shorter survival than classical-BSE-inoculated animals (Comoy et al. 2008). These bear on the human risk of atypical BSE, not on its origin.

The first atypical cattle phenotype

T1Verified at sourcePeer-reviewedLoad-bearing
Biacabe AG, Laplanche JL, Ryder S, Baron T. "Distinct molecular phenotypes in bovine prion diseases." EMBO Reports 2004;5(1):110–115. doi:10.1038/sj.embor.7400054 · PMC1298965
"it has been speculated that a spontaneous rare sporadic form of these diseases could exist in cattle as in humans, and might have been the origin of the BSE epidemic… this hypothesis might also explain our finding"
What it shows
Three cattle in France, among cases found by routine abattoir and rendering-plant testing of animals over 24 months, carried a protease-resistant prion protein pattern unlike classical BSE — a higher molecular mass of the unglycosylated band. The paper does not use the term "H-type"; that name came later.
What it does not show
That the phenotype arises spontaneously. The paper offers that as one of two candidate explanations, in a section headed SPECULATION, and names the other: experimental infection of cattle with a British natural sheep scrapie source "led to similar differences in the PrPres electrophoretic profiles compared to typical cattle BSE". It closes by saying "further studies are now required to determine the frequency of such novel molecular phenotypes in cattle and the biological features of the involved infecting strain."
Load-bearing note
this entry's claim is that atypical BSE arises spontaneously. The paper usually cited as its origin raises that idea as labelled speculation with a scrapie alternative beside it, on three animals.

Where it stands

  • Shown. Atypical BSE exists as two molecular forms distinct from classical BSE, found mainly in older cattle under active surveillance. One H-type case had a heritable PRNP mutation.
  • Still open. Whether cases without a known mutation arise spontaneously, or from an unidentified source. The international framework records the spontaneous origin as a belief, not a finding.
  • a stable age-specific incidence in large herds with no possible feed exposure, across decades and feed histories
  • exposure excluded, or a heritable or de novo cause identified, in field cases outside the E211K family
  • a published critique of the spontaneous hypothesis, which does not yet exist

Atypical BSE exists as two distinct forms → established. Spontaneous origin → a working assumption in trade rules; its own sources say "believed" and "mainly speculative." The test that would settle it (negative tests across millions of unexposed older cattle) → not run, and hard to run.
